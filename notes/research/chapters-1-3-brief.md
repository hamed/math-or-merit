# Chapters I-III research brief

Source-cleared narrative decisions for the opening, fair-game reveal, retrospective story,
and distribution lessons. This brief is ready to guide a prose pass. Four presentation
questions still require the formative study in `reader-study.md`.

## 1. Trillionaire hook

Use a project-authored text card rather than a screenshot or copied news layout:

> June 2026: Elon Musk's estimated net worth crosses $1 trillion.

Place **on paper** in the immediate follow-up and link the dated CBS or Reuters report
already recorded in `narrative-sources.md`. State that the estimate is dominated by
priced shares and options and can move with markets.

**Allowed use:** topical question and scale-setting.

**Not allowed:** claiming the Yard-Sale model explains Musk's fortune, or treating an
estimated net worth as cash in a bank account.

## 2. Merit and hustle setup

Use a project-authored text treatment, not a fabricated social-media screenshot. One
short quotation from Musk's November 2018 post is sufficient:

> "There are way easier places to work, but nobody ever changed the world on 40 hours a week."

Attribute it to Musk, date it, and link the original post. Paraphrase the follow-up as
roughly 80 sustained hours with occasional peaks above 100; do not stack another long
quotation.

Counter with the narrow empirical point supported by the ILO:

> Long hours are common. Working poverty still exists.

This establishes that long work and wealth are not equivalent. It does not claim that
effort has no effect on any individual's life.

Sandel supplies the philosophical bridge: successful people can read outcomes as proof
of talent and hard work while assistance, social conditions, and good fortune recede from
view. Paraphrase and cite the excerpt. Michael Young's satirical origin of
"meritocracy" belongs in a note or later aside, not necessarily the opening flow.

## 3. Spherical cow

Use an explicitly project-authored version because no canonical chemist-versus-physicist
script was established:

> A farmer asks a chemist and a physicist to explain a cow. The chemist returns with
> pages of biology. The physicist draws one circle: "First, assume a spherical cow in a
> vacuum."

The punchline teaches removal of detail. The next sentence must also teach the limit:

> A simpler model can isolate one mechanism. It cannot tell us that every omitted detail
> is irrelevant.

The visual should be project-authored. No external cow illustration is required.

## 4. From Musk to one variable

Morph the authored likeness or symbolic headline portrait into a circle, then say what
the model discards: biography, institutions, work, inheritance, power, and every personal
trait. The retained state is non-negative wealth.

The baseline must not imply that the discarded variables are unimportant in reality. It
asks a narrower question: what can this one fair exchange mechanism do without them?

## 5. Wealth as circle size

Use area proportional to wealth:

```text
radius = scale * sqrt(wealth)
```

Radius proportional to wealth would make area proportional to wealth squared. Because
people compare area imprecisely, circles carry identity and visible change while a common
axis, labels, histogram, or metric carries precise comparison. Minimum radius, maximum
radius, and crowding remain prototype decisions.

## 6. Fair game and winner story

Keep the established exact rule and narrow definition of fairness. Assign arbitrary
visual traits before each run, but keep them out of pairing, stakes, and coin outcomes.

After a winner emerges, deliberately select a conspicuous pre-existing trait and offer a
success story. Then disclose the selection procedure and causal inputs. The safe line is:

> Watch how easily a result invites a story.

Do not name the interaction as a demonstration of outcome bias, hindsight bias, or a
universal attribution error. It is an authored illustration until a participant study
supports a narrower construct.

## 7. Histogram, log scale, and Gini

### Histogram

Use the face-to-bins histogram as the first teaching view because it preserves object
continuity: the reader watches known participants form the bars. State the bin rule. Keep
the cumulative or Lorenz view for comparison and Gini rather than making it the first
chart. This ordering remains subject to the reader test.

### Log scale

Show linear and logarithmic positions side by side, using wealth relative to starting
wealth so multiplicative comparisons have a concrete reference. Teach equal ratios, not
only counting zeros. Put exact or display-level zeros in a separate depleted bucket; do
not place zero on a logarithmic axis.

The log view reveals values hidden by a linear scale. It does **not** reveal or prove a
power law. Omit "power law" from the baseline arc.

### Gini

Teach Gini as a whole-room summary after the reader understands the distribution:

- `0` means everyone has equal wealth;
- for `N` non-negative agents, one owner gives `(N - 1) / N` under the project metric;
- with 100 agents, the maximum is `0.99`, not exactly `1`;
- a realized trade can lower Gini even though the unlimited baseline converges to
  concentration.

Keep top share beside Gini as the intuitive audit.
